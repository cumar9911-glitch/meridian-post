How UK general elections work, and the changes coming: votes at 16 and bank cards as voter ID

650 constituencies, one vote each and the winner takes the seat. We explain first past the post, who can vote, the five-year limit on a Parliament, the photo ID rules, and what the Representation of the People Bill would change.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

The UK's next general election may still be some way off, but the rules for it are changing. A bill now going through Parliament would let 16- and 17-year-olds vote and allow bank cards as voter ID. This explainer covers how general elections work today and what the government proposes to change, drawing on GOV.UK, the law itself, and the government's published summaries of the bill.

The basics: 650 seats, one MP each

A general election chooses the 650 Members of Parliament (MPs) who sit in the House of Commons. The UK is divided into 650 constituencies, and each elects one MP.

MPs are elected by first past the post. As GOV.UK puts it, you vote once for a candidate in your constituency, and the candidate with the most votes becomes your MP. A candidate does not need a majority of votes, only more than anyone else.

How a government is formed

There is no separate vote for prime minister. The government is formed by whoever can command the confidence of the House of Commons, which normally means the leader of the party with the most MPs. With 650 seats, a party needs 326 for an outright majority. If no party reaches that number, the result is a "hung parliament". The largest party may then try to govern as a minority, or parties may agree a coalition or a looser deal.

That is also why a prime minister can change between elections. If a party changes its leader, the new leader can become prime minister without a general election, as long as they still have the support of the Commons.

How often elections happen

GOV.UK says general elections "usually take place every 5 years". The legal limit is set by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022. Under section 4, if it has not been dissolved earlier, a Parliament "dissolves at the beginning of the day that is the fifth anniversary of the day on which it first met". An election then follows. A prime minister can also ask the monarch to dissolve Parliament earlier, and the timing of an election is often a political choice made well before that five-year limit.

Who can vote now

To vote in a general election today, GOV.UK says you must:

  • be registered to vote
  • be 18 or over on polling day
  • be a British, Irish or qualifying Commonwealth citizen
  • be resident at a UK address, or registered as an overseas voter
  • not be legally excluded from voting

You can vote in person, by post or by proxy, where someone you trust votes on your behalf. You need to register again if you change your name, address or nationality.

Photo ID at polling stations

Since recent changes to the law, voters in Great Britain need to show photo ID to vote in person at UK parliamentary elections, including general elections and by-elections. Different rules apply in Northern Ireland. According to GOV.UK, accepted ID includes:

  • a UK or Northern Ireland photocard driving licence, full or provisional
  • a UK passport, or a passport from an EU country, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein or a Commonwealth country
  • a Blue Badge, a PASS proof-of-age card, or certain military ID cards
  • some older and disabled persons' bus passes and travel cards

The ID must be the original, not a photocopy or a photo on a phone. It can be out of date as long as it still looks like you. If you have no accepted ID, you can apply for a free Voter Authority Certificate.

What the Representation of the People Bill would change

The government introduced the Representation of the People Bill in the House of Commons on 12 February 2026 and reintroduced it on 14 May 2026. Its published policy summaries were last updated on 28 October 2026 to reflect amendments. The main proposals include:

  • Votes at 16. The bill would extend the voting age to 16 for all UK elections, giving 16- and 17-year-olds the same rights as other registered voters. The registration age would be lowered to 14, so young people are already on the register if an election falls on or just after their 16th birthday. The government says there will be strict controls on who can access information about young people on the register.
  • Bank cards as voter ID. UK-issued bank cards would become an accepted form of voter ID in Great Britain, if shown in physical form, in date and in the voter's name as it appears on the register. The government says its research shows the share of the population without accepted ID would fall from 2–4% to less than 1% if bank cards were included.
  • Digital ID. Digital versions of photo ID would have to include a visual security feature, such as a digital hologram or a live clock, so that screenshots cannot be used.

The bill also covers voter registration, political finance, imprints on campaign material, postal and proxy voting, and the Electoral Commission. Until it is passed and comes into force, the current rules apply.

Our analysis: the case for and against first past the post

Supporters of first past the post say it is simple, keeps a direct link between each MP and a local area, and usually produces a clear winner who can govern. Critics point out that a party's share of seats can be very different from its share of the national vote. Smaller parties with support spread thinly across the country can win many votes but few seats, while a party can win a large majority without winning half the votes.

Whatever your view, the practical points are the same. Register to vote, check your photo ID before polling day, and if the bill passes, make sure any 16- or 17-year-olds in your household know they can register too.
