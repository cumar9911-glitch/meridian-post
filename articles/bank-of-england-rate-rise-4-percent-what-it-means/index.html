The Bank of England has raised rates to 4%. What it means for your mortgage, savings and loans

Bank Rate went up by a quarter of a point on 5 November, the first rise since the cutting cycle began in 2024. We explain why, who feels it first, and what to watch before the next decision on 17 December.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

On 5 November 2026 the Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4%. It was a small move, a quarter of a percentage point, but it changed direction. The Bank had been cutting rates since August 2024, when they stood at 5.25%, and reached 3.75% in December 2025. This explainer sets out why the Bank changed course, how the decision reaches household budgets, and what the Bank itself says about what comes next.

What exactly was decided

Bank Rate is set by the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), a group of nine people who meet eight times a year. At the meeting that ended on 4 November, eight members voted to raise the rate by 0.25 percentage points to 4%. The ninth wanted to go further, to 4.25%. Nobody voted to hold.

The Bank's job, set by the government, is to keep inflation at 2%. Consumer price inflation (CPI) was 3.5% in September, according to the Office for National Statistics, up from 3.1% in August. The Bank now expects inflation to climb to around 5% by the middle of 2027 before falling back.

Why rates went up when the economy is not in trouble

The short answer is energy. The MPC's summary says the conflict in the Middle East is "now in its ninth month" and that global energy prices "have continued to rise from an already elevated level". In the weeks before the meeting, front-month Brent crude oil futures averaged $108 a barrel and UK gas futures 216 pence per therm, which the minutes say were 38% and 81% higher than at the time of the Bank's July forecast.

The Bank is open about the limits of its power here. Its own explainer says plainly that monetary policy "cannot affect global energy prices". What it is trying to prevent is the second stage of an energy shock: firms passing higher costs on in the price of everything else, and workers pushing for higher pay to keep up, so that a temporary jump in fuel and bills turns into lasting inflation.

So far, the minutes say, there is "limited evidence" of those knock-on effects. Many firms are still protected by fixed-price energy contracts or are absorbing costs through lower profit margins. But the committee judged that this protection is temporary. It also noted that the economy has been stronger than expected, which gives firms more room to raise prices. Put together, the Bank decided that acting now, by a small amount, was less risky than waiting.

How Bank Rate reaches your bank account

Bank Rate is the interest the Bank of England pays to commercial banks and building societies on the money they hold with it, and the rate it charges if it lends to them. Because it sets the price of money at the centre of the system, it influences the rates banks offer everyone else. The effect is not identical for every product, and it is not always immediate.

  • Tracker mortgages are linked directly to Bank Rate, usually as Bank Rate plus a set margin. These borrowers typically see their monthly payment rise from the next payment after the lender applies the change.
  • Standard variable rate (SVR) mortgages are set by each lender. They often move after a Bank Rate change, but lenders decide by how much and when.
  • Fixed-rate mortgages do not change until the fixed period ends. For the many households on fixed deals, the real question is what rates will be on offer when their deal expires. Those rates are priced from market expectations of where Bank Rate is heading, not just today's level.
  • Savings accounts may pay a little more, but banks are not obliged to pass on increases, and easy-access rates often move more slowly than mortgage rates.
  • Credit cards and personal loans are less sensitive to Bank Rate. Their rates mainly reflect the lender's view of the risk that the money will not be repaid.

What a quarter-point rise looks like in pounds

The Bank of England uses a simple example in its guide to interest rates: a £130,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years costs £583 a month at 2.5% interest and £651 at 3.5%. A full percentage point makes a difference of about £68 a month on that loan.

Using the same standard repayment formula, we worked out a smaller change. On a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years, moving from 4.25% to 4.5%, a quarter of a point, raises the monthly payment from about £1,083 to about £1,112, roughly £28 a month or £338 a year. These are our illustrative calculations, not a quote from any lender; your own figures will depend on your balance, term and deal.

For savers the arithmetic runs the other way. A quarter-point higher rate on £10,000 of savings is worth about £25 a year before tax, if the bank passes the whole increase on.

Why higher rates are supposed to bring inflation down

The Bank explains the mechanism in everyday terms. When borrowing costs more, households with variable mortgages and loans have less to spend on other things, saving becomes more rewarding, and firms think twice about borrowing to invest. Lower spending makes it harder for businesses to raise prices, which slows inflation. The effect takes time, which is why the Bank sets rates according to where it expects inflation to be in a year or two, not where it is today.

What the Bank says about the next move

The Bank's summary states that monetary policy "is not on a pre-set path" and that the committee will judge each meeting on the evidence, particularly whether high energy prices are feeding through to wages and prices more widely. Governor Andrew Bailey, presenting the decision, said the increase "shouldn't be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes".

In other words, there is no promise either way. If the conflict eases and energy prices fall back, the pressure for more rises fades. If prices stay high for longer, or businesses start passing costs on more aggressively, the committee has signalled it is prepared to act again.

What to watch

  • 18 November: the ONS publishes October's inflation figures.
  • 17 December: the Bank's next rate decision.
  • Energy markets: oil and gas prices remain, in the Bank's own words, the "main source of uncertainty" for the UK inflation outlook.

If your fixed-rate mortgage ends in the next six months, it is worth checking the deals on offer now. Many lenders let you secure a new rate several months in advance, and an independent mortgage adviser can compare the market for you. This article is general information, not financial advice.
