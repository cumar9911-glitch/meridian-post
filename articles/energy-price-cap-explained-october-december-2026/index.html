The energy price cap explained: what £1,723 really means for your bills this winter

Ofgem's cap rose 4% on 1 October, while VAT on electricity was cut to zero. We explain what the cap limits, what it does not, why wholesale gas drives it, and what the Budget changed for 2027.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Since 1 October 2026 the energy price cap for a typical household in Great Britain paying by Direct Debit has been £1,723 a year, up 4% from £1,663 over the summer. That number appears in almost every story about energy bills. It is also widely misunderstood: it is not the most anyone can pay. This explainer sets out what the cap is, how it is calculated, why it has gone up, and what the government announced in the October Budget.

What the cap actually limits

The energy regulator Ofgem sets the price cap. It limits two things on standard variable tariffs:

  • the unit rate, the price you pay for each kilowatt hour (kWh) of gas and electricity you use, and
  • the standing charge, a fixed daily amount you pay even on days you use no energy.

Ofgem is explicit that the cap "does not limit the cost of your total bill". The more energy you use, the more you pay. The £1,723 figure is simply what a household using a "typical" amount of energy would pay over a year at the capped rates. Ofgem currently defines typical use as 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year.

Your own cap level also depends on where you live, how you pay (Direct Debit, standard credit or prepayment meter) and the type of meter you have.

Who is protected and who is not

The cap protects people on standard variable tariffs, where prices can move up or down. You are not covered if you have agreed a fixed tariff with your supplier, have a business energy contract, get your heat from a heat network, or use heating oil.

The October to December 2026 figures

Ofgem's update, published on 26 August, set these annual levels for typical use:

  • Direct Debit: £1,723 (up from £1,663)
  • Standard credit (paying on receipt of a bill): £1,861 (up from £1,796)
  • Prepayment meter: £1,678 (up from £1,620)
  • Economy 7 on Direct Debit: £1,046 (up from £1,039)

What goes into the cap

Ofgem builds the cap from several cost blocks. For the October to December period, on the Direct Debit cap:

  • Wholesale energy: 47% of the total, up from 44%
  • Networks (the pipes and wires that carry energy): 24%
  • Operating, debt and industry costs: 17%
  • Policy costs (government social and environmental schemes): 6%
  • Supplier profit allowance (earnings before interest and tax): 2.7%
  • Headroom for uncertain costs: 1%
  • Levelisation (so prepayment and Direct Debit customers pay the same standing charge): 0.5%
  • VAT on gas: 5%, while VAT on electricity has been cut to 0%

Why it went up

The biggest driver was wholesale costs. Ofgem said the wholesale allowance rose by 11% and that "continued conflict and geopolitical instability in the Middle East" was the primary driver of market movements. Hot weather across Britain and Europe added to the pressure, and low wind generation meant more reliance on gas-fired power stations.

That last point explains why electricity bills rise when gas gets more expensive. As the Bank of England explains, gas power stations often set the price of electricity in Britain's market, so when gas costs more, so does power. Ofgem's update shows the gas wholesale allowance up 13% and the electricity wholesale allowance up 10% on the previous quarter.

The VAT cut on electricity

The rise would have been bigger without a government decision to remove VAT from electricity bills. Ofgem's letter says VAT on all electricity bills, including those under the cap, falls from 5% to zero from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. VAT on gas stays at 5%.

What the Budget changed for 2027

In the Budget on 28 October, the Chancellor, John Healey, announced several measures on energy bills. According to the Treasury's summary:

  • The temporary VAT cut on electricity will be extended until 1 July 2027, which the government says takes around £50 off the Ofgem annual price cap in April 2027.
  • A £100 Household Energy Payment will be made in 2027 to up to 20 million households in council tax bands A to D in England, with funding for similar support in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • The government will ask Ofgem to abolish gas standing charges and move those costs into unit rates. The Treasury says households that do not see an automatic saving will still get more control over their bills, and that it will set out "targeted mitigation" later.
  • The planned levy on gas bills to fund hydrogen projects will be scrapped, with those projects publicly funded instead.

These measures are paid for in part by raising the Bank Corporation Tax Surcharge from 3% to 6% for 15 months.

Our analysis: what this means in practice

Three things stand out. First, the headline cap is an average, so a large or poorly insulated home can pay well above £1,723 while a small flat pays much less. Checking your own annual usage in kWh, which is on your bill or app, is the only way to know your real exposure.

Second, the cap moves every three months and follows wholesale prices with a lag. Ofgem publishes each new level several weeks before it takes effect. The October level, for example, was announced on 26 August. So the conflict's effect on gas prices this autumn will show up in future caps rather than immediately.

Third, removing gas standing charges would shift costs from low users to high users. Households that use little gas could gain, while large users could pay more per unit, which is presumably why the government has said it will look at targeted help.

What you can do

  • Submit regular meter readings, or make sure your smart meter is sending them, so you are billed for what you actually use.
  • Compare fixed deals against the cap. A fix can bring certainty, but it is only cheaper if prices rise above the fixed rate during the deal.
  • If you are struggling to pay, contact your supplier early. Ofgem expects suppliers to help customers in difficulty, for example with affordable repayment plans.
