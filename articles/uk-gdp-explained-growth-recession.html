UK GDP explained: what 0.7% growth tells us, and what a "recession" actually is

The economy grew 0.7% between July and September 2026, faster than expected despite the energy shock. We explain how GDP is measured, why first estimates get revised, and why "two quarters of decline" is only a rule of thumb.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

On 12 November the Office for National Statistics (ONS) published its first estimate of how the UK economy performed between July and September 2026. The headline was better than many had feared: real gross domestic product (GDP) grew by 0.7%. This explainer looks at what GDP measures, what the latest figures show, and how to read the word "recession" when it appears in headlines.

What GDP measures

GDP is the value of all the goods and services produced in the UK over a period. It is the most widely used measure of the size of the economy and how fast it is growing. "Real" GDP strips out the effect of rising prices, so growth reflects more stuff actually being produced, not just higher prices.

The ONS measures GDP in three ways, which should in theory give the same answer:

  • Output: adding up what every industry produces, from services and manufacturing to construction.
  • Expenditure: adding up what is spent on final goods and services by households, government and businesses (investment), plus exports minus imports.
  • Income: adding up incomes earned from production, such as wages and company profits.

In practice the three measures never match exactly, so the ONS balances them. For the most recent quarters it leans on output data, because more of that information is available quickly.

What the latest figures show

According to the ONS first quarterly estimate for July to September 2026:

  • Real GDP grew by 0.7% in the quarter, after 0.5% growth between April and June.
  • GDP was 1.9% higher than a year earlier.
  • Services grew by 0.9%, led by business-facing services. Production, which includes manufacturing and energy, edged up 0.1%. Construction fell 0.7%.
  • On the spending side, real household consumption rose 0.6%, government consumption rose 0.6%, and business investment rose 0.4%, putting it 3.0% higher than a year earlier.
  • Real GDP per head, which divides GDP by the population, rose 0.7% in the quarter and 1.7% on a year earlier.

The ONS's monthly figures, published the same day, show growth of 0.4% in July, none in August, and 0.2% in September. The ONS said this was the ninth consecutive period of growth on its three-month-on-three-month measure.

Why GDP per head matters

Headline GDP can grow simply because the population is growing: more people working and spending produces more output. GDP per head adjusts for that. The ONS describes it as a proxy for living standards rather than production, because it captures the volume of goods and services available to the average person. In this release both measures grew at a similar pace in the latest quarter.

First estimates are just that

Early GDP figures are based on incomplete data and are revised as more information arrives, sometimes years later. The ONS publishes analysis of how large these revisions tend to be. Since early 2000, the first estimate of quarterly growth has on average been revised up by 0.08 percentage points by the time the final figure appears three years later. The average size of revisions, whether up or down, is 0.27 percentage points.

In other words, a first estimate of 0.7% is a good guide, but it is not exact. The August figure in this release, for example, was revised down from 0.1% growth to zero.

What is a recession?

You will often read that a recession means "two consecutive quarters of falling GDP". The ONS calls this a "technical" recession and accepts it as a widely held international convention. It also points out its weaknesses. The rule ignores how deep the falls are and the wider context, so two tiny dips count as a recession while a single very large fall does not.

The ONS says the convention dates back to American presidential speechwriters in the 1960s. In the United States, recessions are not called by the statistics office at all. The National Bureau of Economic Research looks at the depth, how widespread the decline is (diffusion) and how long it lasts, and describes a recession as "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months".

For that reason, the ONS says that when growth stalls it looks beyond quarterly movements to cumulative changes in GDP and to other economic indicators, rather than relying on one headline number.

Our analysis: strong growth, but the outlook depends on energy

The third-quarter numbers suggest the economy was more resilient than expected through the summer, which the Bank of England also noted when it raised interest rates on 5 November. The Bank said activity had been "stronger than expected", and that this added to the risk of higher energy costs feeding into other prices.

The Office for Budget Responsibility's latest forecast, however, expects higher energy prices to weigh on demand, with spare capacity in the economy widening at the end of 2026. So the question for the next few releases is whether household spending holds up as energy bills, fuel and mortgage costs rise this winter.

What to watch

  • 11 December: the ONS's monthly GDP estimate for October.
  • 22 December: the next quarterly GDP release, which revises the July to September figures.
  • Construction and production: both were weak spots in this release.
