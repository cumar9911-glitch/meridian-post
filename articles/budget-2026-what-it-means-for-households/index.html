Budget 2026: the main changes for households, explained

Energy help, fuel duty, the minimum wage, tax on savings, help for first-time buyers and a new tax allowance for pensioners. A clear guide to what John Healey's 28 October Budget changes, and when.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

The Chancellor, John Healey, delivered the Budget on Wednesday 28 October 2026 against a difficult background: higher oil and gas prices caused by the war in the Middle East, inflation back up to 3.5%, and higher government borrowing costs. The Treasury summed it up as "breathing space for today". This guide sets out the measures most likely to affect household finances, with dates, based on the Treasury's own summary and the Chancellor's speech. Where a policy still needs detailed rules or legislation, we say so.

Energy bills

  • £100 Household Energy Payment in 2027 for up to 20 million households in council tax bands A to D in England. The Treasury says funding for similar support will be provided to the rest of the UK.
  • VAT on electricity stays at zero until 1 July 2027. The temporary cut began on 1 October 2026. The government says the extension takes around £50 off Ofgem's annual price cap in April 2027.
  • Gas standing charges. The government will ask Ofgem to abolish them and move the cost into unit rates. This would help low users most. The Treasury says it will set out "targeted mitigation" for households that do not save automatically.
  • No new policy costs on bills until a new long-term framework is in place, unless they bring bills down or are essential to keep the lights on. A planned levy on gas bills to fund hydrogen projects is scrapped.

These measures are funded partly by raising the Bank Corporation Tax Surcharge, an extra tax on bank profits, from 3% to 6% for 15 months, ending on 31 March 2028. The Treasury expects it to raise £1.5 billion.

Driving

  • Fuel duty: the 5p cut in fuel duty is extended for another six months, and the planned increase in line with the Retail Prices Index (RPI) is deferred to 1 July 2027.
  • Mileage allowances: tax-free mileage rates for people who use their own car for work will be made permanently more generous. The Treasury says this helps around 2 million employees and 1 million self-employed people.
  • Electric vehicles: the government plans to cut prices at local, lower-powered public chargers, which it says will save drivers who rely on them £150 a year on average.

Work and pay

  • National Living Wage: rises by 3.9% to £13.21 an hour from 1 April 2027. The Treasury says that is worth £910 a year before tax to a full-time worker.
  • Parental pay: extended to more than 160,000 additional parents, including self-employed fathers and partners who previously missed out.

Savings, property income and ISAs

  • Tax on savings and property income will rise by a further two percentage points across all bands from April 2027. The Treasury says 84% of taxpayers will see no increase in their tax bill.
  • The £20,000 cash ISA limit is kept for everyone.
  • First-time buyers: a new First Time Buyer ISA is planned, and from Budget day the Lifetime ISA withdrawal charge is cut to 20%. The Treasury says this means first-time buyers purchasing a home above the Lifetime ISA's £450,000 property price cap will not be penalised.
  • Your First Home Scheme: the government says it will help 70,000 people onto the property ladder.

Pensioners

  • Pension Tax Allowance from April 2027. People over State Pension age whose total income is no more than the value of the full new State Pension will not pay income tax. The Treasury estimates around 500,000 pensioners will benefit in 2027-28, with about 290,000 paying no income tax at all and the rest getting a tapered allowance.
  • The triple lock. The Chancellor repeated the Prime Minister's announcement that from 2030 the triple lock will be "adjusted" so that the State Pension keeps pace with earnings and inflation. He said this would save £15 billion a year by the end of the 2030s. The detail has not yet been set out. See our triple lock explainer.

Shopping and the high street

  • Tariffs suspended on more than 100 products. The Chancellor said this was to help with the weekly shop. The Treasury's examples include baked beans and chocolate, alongside tariff cuts on kerosene and fertiliser.
  • Business rates in England: retail, hospitality and leisure properties will see bills frozen in real terms, reduced or removed. The government says around 70,000 more of these businesses will be taken out of business rates by raising the Small Business Rates Relief threshold.
  • Online marketplaces will have to collect VAT on qualifying sales by UK businesses from October 2027, a measure aimed at evasion.

The bigger picture: borrowing and defence

The Chancellor said the Budget meets his two fiscal rules: day-to-day spending covered by tax receipts (the "stability rule"), and debt, measured as public sector net financial liabilities, falling as a share of GDP (the "investment rule"). He said the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) puts headroom against the stability rule at £15 billion in 2029-30. The OBR's latest guide to the public finances forecasts a deficit of £133 billion in 2026-27, or 4.2% of national income.

On defence, the Treasury says spending will reach 2.7% of GDP in 2027-28, with a target of 3% in 2030 and plans for meeting NATO's 3.5% commitment by 2035 to come at next year's Spending Review.

Our analysis

This was a Budget mostly about cushioning a price shock that the Treasury cannot control. The biggest household measures, the £100 payment, the electricity VAT cut and the fuel duty freeze, are temporary and run to mid-2027. Their value to any one household depends on its council tax band, energy use and driving.

The longer-term changes deserve closer attention. Higher tax rates on savings and property income will mainly affect landlords and people with large savings outside ISAs. The plan to scrap gas standing charges could shift costs between households, depending on the detailed design. And the triple lock change is one of the most significant pension policy shifts in years, yet the formula has not been published.

As always with Budgets, many measures need legislation, usually through a Finance Bill, or detailed rules before they take effect. The dates above are the government's stated plans.
