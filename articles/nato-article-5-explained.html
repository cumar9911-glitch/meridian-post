NATO's Article 5 explained: what "an attack on one is an attack on all" really commits countries to

Article 5 is the heart of the NATO alliance, but it does not automatically send troops to war. We explain the exact wording, how it is triggered, the one time it was used, and the new 5% defence spending pledge.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

"An attack on one is an attack on all" is probably the most famous line in international security. It sums up Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, the promise at the centre of NATO. With Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine now in its fifth year, conflict in the Middle East and the UK committing to spend more on defence, the clause is discussed more than at any time since the Cold War. But what does it actually say, and what does it oblige countries to do?

Where Article 5 comes from

The North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington, D.C. on 4 April 1949 by 12 countries from Europe and North America. NATO notes it is a short document: 14 articles and just over 1,000 words. Its purpose was a joint pledge, at the start of the Cold War, that each country would help the others if attacked, to deter the Soviet Union from extending its control westward.

The exact words

Article 5 states:

"The Parties agree that an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all and consequently they agree that, if such an armed attack occurs, each of them, in exercise of the right of individual or collective self-defence recognized by Article 51 of the Charter of the United Nations, will assist the Party or Parties so attacked by taking forthwith, individually and in concert with the other Parties, such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force, to restore and maintain the security of the North Atlantic area."

It adds that any such attack, and the measures taken in response, must be reported to the UN Security Council, and that those measures end when the Security Council has acted to restore peace.

What it does, and does not, require

The key phrase is "such action as it deems necessary". Each member must help, but each decides how. NATO says assistance "may or may not involve the use of armed force". That flexibility was deliberate. According to NATO, when the treaty was drafted, European countries wanted a guarantee that the United States would automatically send forces if they were attacked. The US did not want to make such a specific pledge, and the looser wording was the compromise.

Article 11 of the treaty also recognises that countries have their own constitutional processes. Some, for example, need parliamentary approval before deploying forces abroad.

So Article 5 is a binding obligation to assist, not an automatic declaration of war by every member.

What counts as an "armed attack"

The obvious case is one state invading another. But NATO says what counts must be judged case by case and is not limited to traditional military strikes by a state. At recent summits, NATO leaders have said Article 5 can apply to attacks to, from or within space, and that significant cyber attacks and other "hybrid" attacks may be treated as armed attacks. Purely domestic incidents, such as home-grown terrorism with no international element, do not trigger it.

There is also a geographic limit. Article 6 restricts the obligation mainly to the North Atlantic area north of the Tropic of Cancer.

How it is triggered

There is no set procedure in the treaty. NATO says the obligation arises when two conditions are met: a member has suffered an armed attack, and that member asks for, or agrees to, collective action. An attacked country can choose to handle a situation another way.

In practice, members would meet urgently in the North Atlantic Council, NATO's main political decision-making body, to decide whether the attack is covered by Article 5.

People often confuse Article 5 with Article 4. Article 4 lets any member call for consultations when it believes its territorial integrity, political independence or security is threatened. NATO stresses that Article 4 consultations are neither required before Article 5 nor a sign that Article 5 is being considered.

The only time it has been used

NATO has invoked Article 5 once, after the terrorist attacks on the United States on 11 September 2001. The next evening the North Atlantic Council agreed that if the attack had been directed from abroad, it would be covered by Article 5. On 2 October 2001, after a briefing on the investigation, it confirmed that it was.

Two days later, allies agreed eight measures to support the United States. These included more intelligence sharing, blanket overflight clearances, access to ports and airfields, and deploying NATO naval forces to the eastern Mediterranean. NATO then launched Operation Eagle Assist, in which seven NATO AWACS radar aircraft helped patrol US skies from October 2001 to May 2002, with crews from 13 countries flying more than 360 sorties. A second operation, Active Endeavour, patrolled the Mediterranean until 2016.

Deterrence and spending: Article 3

NATO argues that Article 5 works mainly by deterring attacks in the first place. That depends on Article 3, under which members commit to "maintain and develop" their capacity to resist attack, in other words to spend on defence.

At the Hague summit in June 2025, NATO leaders committed to invest 5% of GDP a year by 2035 on defence and security. The Hague declaration says this splits into at least 3.5% on core defence, using NATO's definition, and up to 1.5% on related areas such as protecting critical infrastructure, cyber defence, civil preparedness and the defence industry. The path is to be reviewed in 2029.

In the October 2026 Budget, the UK government said defence spending would reach 2.7% of GDP in 2027-28. It set a target of 3% in 2030 and said it would explain at next year's Spending Review how it will meet the 3.5% commitment by 2035.

Our analysis

Article 5's strength has never been its legal detail. Its strength is the credibility of the promise behind it, backed by joint planning, exercises and forces stationed on NATO's eastern flank. Its flexibility is both a feature and a weakness. It lets each country respond in a way its own parliament can support, but it also means that how much help an ally would get is ultimately a political decision. That is why debates about defence spending and political commitment are, in the end, debates about Article 5.
