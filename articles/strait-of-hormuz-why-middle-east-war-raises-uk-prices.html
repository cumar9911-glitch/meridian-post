The Strait of Hormuz: why a war in the Gulf is pushing up UK prices

About a quarter of the world's seaborne oil trade passes through one narrow channel off Iran. We explain how disruption there feeds into petrol, energy bills, inflation and mortgage rates in Britain.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Britain imports relatively little oil directly from the Gulf. So why does a conflict involving Iran show up so quickly on UK petrol station signs, energy bills and even mortgage rates? The answer lies in a narrow stretch of water and in the way global energy prices are set. This analysis draws on figures from the International Energy Agency (IEA), the Bank of England, Ofgem and the Office for National Statistics.

A very narrow chokepoint

The Strait of Hormuz separates the Arabian Peninsula from Iran and connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. At its narrowest it is only 21 nautical miles (39km) wide, and the shipping lanes themselves are two-mile-wide channels in each direction with a buffer zone between them.

According to the IEA, an average of 20 million barrels a day of crude oil and oil products passed through the strait in 2025. That is around 25% of the world's seaborne oil trade. The strait is the main export route for oil from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq, Bahrain and Iran.

It matters for gas too. The IEA says about 93% of Qatar's and 96% of the UAE's liquefied natural gas (LNG) exports go through the strait, together about 19% of global LNG trade.

There are some alternatives, but not many. Saudi Arabia and the UAE have pipelines that can carry crude to ports outside the Gulf. The IEA estimates 3.5 to 5.5 million barrels a day of spare capacity on those routes, well short of the 20 million that normally sail through the strait.

Britain buys little Gulf oil, but pays the world price

Most of the oil leaving the strait goes east. The IEA says around 80% is destined for Asia, with China, India and Japan the biggest buyers, and only about 4% of the region's crude flows to Europe.

That might suggest the UK is insulated. It is not, because oil and gas are traded in global markets. When a large share of world supply is disrupted, buyers everywhere compete for the barrels and cargoes that remain, and the price rises for everyone, including oil from the North Sea, Norway or the United States. LNG cargoes work in a similar way: if Asian buyers lose Qatari supply, they bid for cargoes that might otherwise have come to Europe.

The Bank of England puts it simply in its explainer on the current rise in interest rates. The number of ships moving through the strait "has fallen by a large amount", and when supplies cannot reach their destination, "supply goes down but demand stays the same", so prices rise.

How big the price moves have been

The Bank's Monetary Policy Committee reported that in the 15 UK working days to 27 October, Brent crude futures averaged $108 a barrel and UK gas futures averaged 216 pence per therm, 38% and 81% higher respectively than at the time of the Bank's July forecast. In his Budget speech on 28 October, the Chancellor, John Healey, said oil had risen "from around $70 a barrel to well over $100" and that gas prices had "nearly tripled since February".

The Bank also notes that prices have been volatile. They rose when the conflict broke out, fell back during temporary easing in hostilities, and climbed again when fighting resumed.

Four routes from the Gulf to your household budget

1. At the pump. Petrol and diesel prices follow crude oil prices most directly. The ONS reported that in September 2026 petrol averaged 169.5p a litre, the highest since August 2022, and diesel 192.5p, the highest since July 2022. Motor fuels made the largest upward contribution to the rise in inflation that month.

2. Gas and electricity bills. Wholesale costs are the biggest single part of Ofgem's price cap, 47% of it for October to December. Ofgem said "continued conflict and geopolitical instability in the Middle East" was the primary driver of wholesale market movements, and the cap rose 4% (see our price cap explainer). Electricity is affected as well as gas because, as the Bank explains, gas-fired power stations often set the electricity price.

3. Everything else. Energy is an input to almost everything: transport, food processing, manufacturing, heating shops and offices. The Bank estimates energy made up about 8% of household spending in 2024, but its indirect reach is much wider. If firms' costs stay high, they may raise prices, and workers may ask for higher pay to keep up. So far, the Bank says, there is little evidence of these knock-on effects, partly because many firms have fixed-price energy contracts or are absorbing costs in lower profits. It expects them to "eventually kick in" if energy stays expensive.

4. Interest rates. To stop a temporary energy shock turning into lasting inflation, the Bank raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4% on 5 November. It expects CPI inflation, 3.5% in September, to reach around 5% by mid-2027. Higher rates feed through to tracker mortgages and to the price of new fixed-rate deals.

There is also a cost to the public finances

The Chancellor told Parliament that the Office for Budget Responsibility expects the government to spend £14 billion more on debt interest this year, which he linked to higher borrowing costs across the G7 driven by the conflict. In the Budget he extended the 5p cut in fuel duty for six months and extended the temporary removal of VAT from electricity bills to July 2027.

Our analysis: what would bring prices down

The Bank is clear that it cannot influence global energy prices. What happens next depends mainly on events in the region. The Bank's minutes say a "credible and sustained de-escalation" could lead prices to fall, while continued fighting, wider escalation or further damage to refining capacity could keep them higher than markets currently expect. They also note that energy supply buffers were drawn down during 2026, which could make price spikes sharper.

For households, that means the cost of living this winter depends heavily on shipping through a strait 21 nautical miles wide at its narrowest. The timing differs by bill. Fuel prices respond within weeks. Energy bills follow wholesale prices with a lag through the quarterly price cap. The wider effect on other prices, if it comes, will take longer still.
