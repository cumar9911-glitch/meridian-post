UK inflation explained: what CPI, CPIH and "core" inflation actually measure

Inflation rose to 3.5% in September 2026, driven by petrol and diesel. Here is how the figure is calculated, why there are several measures, and how to read the next release on 18 November.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Every month the Office for National Statistics (ONS) publishes a number that shapes mortgage rates, pension increases, benefit uprating and pay negotiations across the country. In September 2026 that number was 3.5%: prices, on the Consumer Prices Index (CPI) measure, were 3.5% higher than a year earlier. But the ONS headline release actually contains several inflation rates, and they tell slightly different stories. This guide explains what each one measures and how to read them.

How inflation is measured

Inflation is the rate at which prices rise. To measure it, the ONS tracks the prices of a large "basket" of goods and services that is designed to represent what households buy. The Bank of England describes it as about 700 items, from a loaf of bread and a bus ticket to cars and holidays. Each item is weighted by how much households spend on it, so a rise in the price of something people buy a lot of, like petrol, moves the index more than a rise in something people rarely buy.

The inflation rate is the change in that index over 12 months. As the Bank's guide puts it, if inflation is 2%, a basket that cost £100 a year ago costs £102 today. Inflation falling does not mean prices are falling; it means they are rising more slowly. Prices actually falling is called deflation.

CPI: the headline measure

The CPI is the measure the government uses for the Bank of England's 2% inflation target, so it is the figure most news reports lead with. In September 2026:

  • CPI inflation was 3.5%, up from 3.1% in August.
  • On a monthly basis, prices rose 0.4% in September, compared with little change a year earlier. That monthly comparison is why the annual rate jumped.

CPIH: CPI plus the cost of owning a home

The CPI leaves out one of the biggest costs many households face: the cost of living in a home you own. CPIH ("H" for housing) adds a measure of the costs of owning, maintaining and living in your own home, which the ONS calls owner occupiers' housing costs, along with council tax. The ONS describes CPIH as the most comprehensive measure of inflation.

The ONS calls CPIH its lead measure of inflation. Owner occupiers' housing costs make up about 18% of it, which is the main reason the two measures differ. In September 2026 CPIH inflation was 3.6%, up from 3.3% in August, and the owner occupiers' housing component on its own rose by 4.0% over the year.

Core inflation: stripping out the most volatile prices

Energy, food, alcohol and tobacco prices can swing sharply because of events far beyond the UK, such as a war, a poor harvest or a tax change. "Core" inflation removes them to show the underlying trend in the rest of the economy. It matters to the Bank of England because it is a better guide to whether price rises are spreading and becoming entrenched.

  • Core CPI rose by 2.8% in the 12 months to September, up from 2.6% in August.
  • Core CPIH rose by 3.1%, up from 2.9%.

The gap between headline (3.5%) and core (2.8%) tells you that a big part of current inflation is coming from the items that are excluded, especially energy.

Goods versus services

The ONS also splits prices into goods (physical things) and services (haircuts, rents, insurance, meals out). Services prices depend heavily on wages, so economists watch them to judge whether higher costs are feeding into pay. In September CPI goods inflation rose from 2.7% to 3.2%, and services inflation rose from 3.4% to 3.7%.

What pushed inflation up in September

The single biggest upward push came from transport, particularly motor fuels. The ONS reported that the average price of petrol rose by 8.2 pence a litre between August and September 2026, to 169.5p, the highest since August 2022. Diesel rose by 10.7p to 192.5p a litre, the highest since July 2022. Annual transport inflation, on the CPIH measure, jumped from 4.6% to 6.6%.

Pulling in the other direction, restaurant and hotel prices rose less than they had a year earlier, and food and non-alcoholic drink inflation stayed low at 1.3%.

The Bank of England says direct energy effects added around 1 percentage point to September's 3.5%, most of it from fuel. It links the rise in energy costs to the conflict in the Middle East, which has disrupted the supply and transport of oil and gas.

Why this matters for your money

  • Interest rates. The Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 4% on 5 November because it expects CPI inflation to rise to around 5% by mid-2027.
  • The state pension. Under the triple lock, the new State Pension rises each year by the highest of earnings growth, CPI inflation or 2.5%.
  • Savings. If your savings earn less than the rate of inflation, their spending power is falling, even though the balance is going up.
  • Pay. "Real" wages, meaning pay adjusted for inflation, only rise if pay growth beats inflation.

How to read the next release

October's figures are due on 18 November 2026. When they come out, it is worth looking past the headline:

  • Compare the monthly change with the same month last year. That tells you whether the annual rate is likely to rise or fall.
  • Check core and services inflation, which show whether price rises are spreading beyond energy.
  • Look at which divisions made the biggest contributions. The ONS publishes this every month, and it shows exactly what is driving the change.

Inflation figures are estimates based on a sample of prices, and the ONS publishes detailed notes on its methods with every release. The headline is a useful summary, but the detail underneath is where you find out what is really happening to the cost of living.
