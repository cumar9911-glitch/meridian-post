Deficit, debt and "headroom": a plain-English guide to the UK's public finances

The government expects to borrow £133 billion this year and spend £124 billion on debt interest. We explain what the deficit and the national debt are, how the fiscal rules work, and why the war in the Middle East matters to the Treasury.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Every Budget comes with a set of numbers that can sound abstract: the deficit, net debt, headroom, fiscal rules. Yet these numbers shape tax rises, spending cuts and how much the government can do in a crisis. This guide explains the main terms, using the latest forecast from the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR), published alongside the Budget in October 2026.

Who the OBR is

The OBR is the UK's independent public finance watchdog, set up in 2010. Twice a year, usually alongside the Budget and the Spring or Autumn Statement, it publishes five-year forecasts for the economy and the public finances. It includes the expected effect of the government's policy decisions and judges whether the government is on course to meet the targets it has set itself. The government's figures in a Budget are the OBR's figures, which is meant to stop Chancellors marking their own homework.

Income: where the money comes from

The OBR expects the public sector to raise £1,316 billion in 2026-27. That is around £45,000 per household, or 41.4% of national income. Taxes make up about 90% of this. The biggest are:

  • Income tax and National Insurance contributions: around £580 billion together
  • VAT: around £186 billion
  • Other significant taxes include corporation tax, council tax, business rates, capital gains tax and fuel duty.

Spending: where it goes

The OBR expects public spending of £1,449 billion in 2026-27, around £50,000 per household, or 45.6% of national income. Among the largest items:

  • Day-to-day spending by government departments: £555 billion, including the Department of Health and Social Care (£212 billion), the Department for Education (£95 billion) and the Ministry of Defence (£42 billion).
  • Welfare: £353 billion, of which roughly half (£170 billion) goes to pensioners, mostly through the State Pension. Universal Credit and the benefits it replaces account for £97 billion.
  • Capital investment in things like roads and buildings: £171 billion.
  • Net interest on the national debt: £124 billion.

The deficit: borrowing this year

When spending is higher than income, the government borrows to cover the gap. That gap is the deficit, officially called public sector net borrowing. The OBR forecasts a deficit of £133 billion in 2026-27, or 4.2% of national income, falling to £68 billion by 2031-32.

Deficits are normal for the UK. The OBR notes that the government has run a surplus in only 10 years since 1948, and only three since 1971-72. The deficit also rises and falls with the economy. When times are good, tax receipts rise and welfare costs fall. When the economy weakens, the reverse happens.

The government's main target uses the current deficit, which leaves out spending on net investment. The idea is that borrowing to build things that will last is more defensible than borrowing to pay running costs. The OBR expects a current deficit of £41 billion in 2026-27, turning into a current surplus of £21 billion by 2031-32.

The debt: what has built up over time

If the deficit is the overspend in one year, the national debt is the total built up over many years. The most watched measure is public sector net debt, which is gross debt minus liquid assets such as foreign exchange reserves. The OBR expects it to be about 95.5% of national income in 2026-27, around £3.1 trillion. It forecasts a peak of 97.3% in 2028-29 before a gradual fall to 95.6% in 2031-32, by which point the cash total would be around £3.7 trillion.

Debt can fall as a share of the economy even while it rises in pounds, as long as the economy grows faster than the debt. That is why the targets are usually expressed as percentages of GDP.

The fiscal rules, and what "headroom" means

The government has set itself two main rules. In his Budget speech on 28 October, the Chancellor, John Healey, described them like this:

  • The stability rule: day-to-day spending must be covered by tax receipts. In OBR terms, the current budget must be in balance by 2029-30.
  • The investment rule: debt, measured as public sector net financial liabilities, must be falling as a share of GDP. This wider measure also counts assets such as student loans. The target year is 2029-30.

Headroom is the margin by which the government is forecast to meet a rule. The OBR says the stability rule was met by £15 billion and the investment rule by £24 billion at the October 2026 forecast. Headroom matters because forecasts change. A small margin can disappear with one bad set of economic data or a rise in interest rates, which can force a Chancellor to raise taxes or cut spending at the next fiscal event.

Why the war in the Middle East matters to the Treasury

Higher energy prices affect the public finances in several ways. The Chancellor told MPs that government borrowing costs across the G7 had reached highs "not seen for decades", and that the OBR expects the UK to spend £14 billion more on debt interest this year. Higher inflation also raises the cost of inflation-linked debt and of benefits that rise with prices. The OBR's forecast expects higher energy prices to weigh on demand, with spare capacity in the economy widening at the end of 2026 and closing by 2031.

The Chancellor said he had used some of the headroom built up last year to absorb the impact of the conflict, through measures such as the fuel duty freeze and the extended VAT cut on electricity, while still meeting the rules.

How the UK compares

Using data from the OECD, the OBR says the UK raises less in revenue than countries such as Germany, Denmark and Norway but slightly more than the US, Japan and Australia. It spends slightly less than the average industrial country. The result is a deficit, and a level of net debt, above the industrial-country average.

Our analysis

Two numbers are worth remembering. The first is £124 billion, the annual cost of debt interest, which is more than the Department for Education's £95 billion day-to-day budget. The second is the headroom, £15 billion against the main rule. That is small compared with the £1.4 trillion the government spends each year. Together they explain why the Treasury is so sensitive to interest rates and energy prices, and why every forecast now gets so much attention.
