How UK sanctions work: asset freezes, trade bans and who has to obey them

Sanctions are one of the main tools of UK foreign policy, from Russia to terrorism. We explain what they are, how people and companies end up on the list, what an asset freeze actually does, and what happens to those who break the rules.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

When the government announces new sanctions, the headlines usually focus on the names: an oligarch, a bank, a shipping company, a military unit. The machinery behind those announcements gets far less attention, yet it reaches deep into ordinary UK business, from banks and lawyers to estate agents and exporters. This explainer sets out how the UK sanctions system works, based on the government's own guidance.

What sanctions are, and why the UK uses them

The government describes sanctions as "restrictive measures" that it imposes on individuals, businesses, organisations and ships. They begin as a policy decision and are then made into law. According to GOV.UK, the UK uses sanctions to:

  • comply with United Nations and other international obligations
  • support foreign policy and national security objectives
  • maintain international peace and security
  • prevent terrorism

The Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) leads on sanctions policy. Enforcement is shared out. The Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI), part of HM Treasury, handles financial sanctions. The Office of Trade Sanctions Implementation deals with trade measures. HMRC and the Department for Transport also have roles.

Regimes: by country or by theme

UK sanctions are organised into "regimes". Some are geographic, aimed at a particular country or area, such as Russia, Libya or the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Others are thematic, aimed at a type of behaviour wherever it happens, such as the UK's domestic counter-terrorism regime. Each regime has its own regulations and its own statutory guidance setting out exactly what is prohibited.

Five types of sanction

According to the government's starter guide, a regime can include any or all of these:

  • Financial sanctions, most commonly asset freezes, plus wider limits on investment and financial services.
  • Director disqualification, which bans a sanctioned person from running or helping to form a UK company.
  • Trade sanctions, which ban the export or import of certain goods and technology, or the provision of related services.
  • Immigration sanctions, such as travel bans.
  • Transport sanctions, covering ships and aircraft.

Who gets sanctioned, and how you find them

Many measures target named "designated persons", a term that covers both individuals and organisations. The regulations for each regime set out the grounds on which the government can designate someone. Under the Libya regime, for example, those grounds include reasonable grounds to suspect involvement in serious human rights abuses in Libya.

Everyone designated, along with ships that have been "specified", appears on the UK Sanctions List. It is public and searchable and can include aliases, dates of birth, nationalities, passport numbers and addresses.

Not all sanctions name names. Sectoral sanctions apply to a whole category of activity. Under the Russia regime, for example, it is prohibited to grant or arrange new loans or credit with a maturity of more than 30 days to a "person connected with Russia". That category is defined to include people ordinarily resident or located in Russia, and companies incorporated or domiciled there. Because these targets are not listed by name, businesses have to read the regulations themselves.

What an asset freeze actually does

An asset freeze is the most common financial sanction. It does not mean the government seizes the money. As the guidance puts it, freezing "means blocking access" to funds and "economic resources", which can include property, vehicles, antiques and precious metals. It generally prohibits:

  • dealing with funds or resources owned, held or controlled by a designated person, even if someone else is holding them
  • making funds or resources available, directly or indirectly, to or for the benefit of a designated person, which includes loans and credit
  • doing anything designed to get around those rules

Businesses that find they hold such assets must freeze them immediately and tell OFSI.

The ownership rule

One of the most important, and least understood, parts of the system is that asset freezes automatically extend to any company that a designated person owns or controls, even if that company is not on the list. The government's example is simple. Company X is not listed, but its majority owner, Company Y, is. Company X is therefore subject to the same restrictions. That is why banks and professional firms carry out "due diligence" on ownership structures, not just name checks.

Who must comply

UK sanctions apply to:

  • anyone carrying out activities anywhere in the UK, including its territorial waters
  • any business or organisation set up under UK law, wherever in the world it operates
  • any UK national, wherever they are

Certain types of firm, including many in financial services, also have legal duties to report what they know about designated persons and suspected breaches.

What happens if someone breaks the rules

Breaching financial sanctions includes dealing with frozen funds or making money available to a designated person without a licence. According to the guidance, an investigation can lead to a criminal prosecution, a civil monetary penalty, a warning letter or a published decision naming the party responsible. OFSI also publishes its enforcement decisions and penalties.

Licences matter too. Activity that would otherwise break financial sanctions can be lawful if OFSI has licensed it. OFSI considers individual licence applications and also publishes general licences that apply more widely.

Our analysis: why sanctions are hard to make bite

Sanctions are only as strong as their enforcement. The rise of complex ownership chains, shell companies and cryptoassets is why OFSI's guidance now includes threat assessments for sectors such as property, legal services, art dealing and crypto, and specific advice on evasion of the Russian oil price cap. For most UK businesses, the practical message from the guidance is clear: check the list, understand who really owns the companies you deal with, and report anything suspicious. Anyone can report a suspected breach through GOV.UK.
