The 2026 US midterms: how Congress elections work and what a Democratic Congress can and cannot do

Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate on 3 November. We explain how midterm elections work, when the new Congress takes over, and what powers it will have over President Trump's final two years.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

American voters went to the polls on Tuesday 3 November 2026 for the midterm elections, held halfway through a presidential term. According to the BBC and Reuters, the Democrats won control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, which means Congress will be in Democratic hands for the rest of Donald Trump's presidency. This explainer sets out how these elections work, what happens next, and what a change of control does and does not mean.

What was on the ballot

Congress, the legislative branch of the US government, has two chambers. The official USAGov guide explains the cycle:

  • The House of Representatives has 435 voting members, each elected for a two-year term to represent a district. Every House seat is up for election every two years. House.gov notes that the number of voting representatives, 435, was set by law in 1911 and has applied since 1913, and that seats are shared out among the states according to population.
  • The Senate has 100 members, two per state, who serve six-year terms. The terms are staggered, so about one-third of the Senate is elected every two years.

In 2026, all 435 House seats were contested, along with a third of the Senate. Many states also elected governors and state legislators, and voted on local ballot measures.

What happened

The BBC reported that the Democrats had won both chambers and picked up Senate seats in Republican strongholds, including Texas, Ohio and Kansas. Reuters reported on 4 November that Democrats had flipped seats in deeply Republican states for a Senate majority of "at least 54-44". It also said voters' anger over the cost of living, including the effect of the US-Iran war on prices, helped the Democrats.

Counting and certification take time in US elections. Some contests, including Alaska's Senate race, which uses ranked-choice voting, were still being counted more than a week after polling day, according to The Independent, so final seat totals may change. Each state certifies its own results.

When the new Congress takes over

Nothing changes immediately. Under the 20th Amendment to the US Constitution, the terms of senators and representatives end, and their successors' terms begin, at noon on 3 January. Until then, the current Congress, where Republicans have held both chambers, remains in place for what is known as the "lame duck" session.

When the new Congress meets in January 2027, the majority party in each chamber chooses its leaders, including the Speaker of the House, and takes the chairs of every committee.

What a Democratic Congress can do

Control of Congress gives the majority real power over the agenda, even with a president from the other party.

  • Set the legislative agenda. The majority decides which bills reach the floor for a vote. The president's legislative priorities will need Democratic votes to pass.
  • Control the money. Congress passes the federal budget and the appropriations bills that fund government departments. This gives the majority leverage in negotiations with the White House, and makes funding deadlines a regular flashpoint.
  • Investigate. Committees can hold hearings, demand documents and question officials. The Independent reported that the president's opponents are expected to use this power to investigate his administration.
  • Confirm or reject appointments. The Senate confirms cabinet members, federal judges, including Supreme Court justices, and ambassadors. A Democratic Senate can slow or block nominees.
  • Approve treaties. Treaties need the approval of two-thirds of the Senate.

What it cannot do

Divided government has limits for both sides.

  • The president keeps the veto. If Congress passes a bill the president opposes, he can veto it. Overriding a veto needs a two-thirds majority in both chambers, which is far larger than the Democrats' expected majorities.
  • The Senate filibuster. Under current Senate rules, most legislation needs 60 votes to end debate and proceed to a final vote. A majority in the mid-50s is not enough to pass most bills on party lines.
  • Executive action continues. Presidents can act through executive orders and agency rules, though Congress can respond through funding decisions and legislation, and courts can review them.

Why it matters outside the US

For the UK and other allies, a divided Washington can affect trade, sanctions, foreign aid and defence policy, because many of these need congressional approval or funding. Policy on the conflict in the Middle East and support for Ukraine will now be shaped by negotiation between a Republican White House and a Democratic Congress.

Our analysis

Midterms often go against the president's party, but the scale of this result, including Senate wins in states that rarely elect Democrats, suggests the economy was decisive. Rising prices linked to the war were central to the campaign. The most likely result is two years of hard bargaining: fewer major laws, tougher fights over government funding, and more congressional investigations. The next big test comes in the budget negotiations after the new Congress is sworn in.
