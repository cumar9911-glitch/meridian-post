How the WHO declares a global health emergency, and the new "pandemic emergency" alert

When an outbreak spreads, the World Health Organization can declare a "public health emergency of international concern". We explain who decides, what it changes, what it cannot do, and the higher alert level added after COVID-19.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

When a dangerous outbreak starts to cross borders, as with the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo that we covered in October, one question comes up quickly: will the World Health Organization (WHO) declare a global health emergency? The phrase sounds dramatic, but it has a precise legal meaning. Since September 2025 there has also been a second, higher level of alert. This explainer sets out how the system works.

The rulebook: the International Health Regulations

The legal framework is the International Health Regulations (2005), known as the IHR. The WHO describes them as a legally binding instrument of international law. According to the WHO's announcement in September 2025, they apply to 196 "States Parties", including all 194 WHO member states. The IHR set out countries' rights and obligations when public health risks could spread internationally.

Among other things, the IHR require countries to:

  • build and maintain core capacity to detect and respond to health threats, including at airports, ports and border crossings
  • notify the WHO of public health events that could have international implications, wherever they come from
  • verify information about health risks that the WHO has picked up from unofficial sources

The WHO traces the system's origins to 19th-century quarantine rules. They became global under the International Sanitary Regulations in 1951, which were later renamed the IHR.

What a PHEIC is

The best-known alert under the IHR is the public health emergency of international concern, or PHEIC. The IHR define it as "an extraordinary event which is determined to constitute a public health risk to other States through the international spread of disease and to potentially require a coordinated international response".

The WHO explains that this implies a situation that is:

  • serious, sudden, unusual or unexpected
  • has implications for public health beyond the affected country's borders
  • may require immediate international action

Who decides

The decision belongs to the WHO Director-General, currently Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus. But the Director-General is advised by an Emergency Committee of international experts.

According to the WHO, committee members come from a roster of experts in fields such as disease control, virology, vaccine development and epidemiology. At least one member should be an expert nominated by the country where the event is happening, and that country is invited to present its views. The names of members, and any potential conflicts of interest, are published. Members are not paid by the WHO for this work.

The committee advises on three questions: whether the event is a PHEIC, what temporary recommendations should apply to the affected country and to others, and when the emergency should end. The Director-General then decides, weighing the committee's advice, information from the countries involved, scientific evidence, and an assessment of the risks to health, of international spread, and of interference with travel and trade.

What a declaration changes

Declaring a PHEIC does not unlock a single pot of money or send in WHO troops. It is a formal signal to the world that a coordinated international response is needed. It comes with temporary recommendations, aimed, in the WHO's words, at preventing or reducing the international spread of disease, for the affected country and for others.

The IHR also aim to avoid "unnecessary interference with international traffic and trade". This reflects a long-standing tension. Countries sometimes impose travel bans that go beyond WHO advice, which can discourage others from reporting outbreaks quickly.

Temporary recommendations expire automatically after three months. Emergency committees therefore meet again at least every three months to review the situation and decide whether the emergency should continue. A statement is published after each meeting.

The new top level: "pandemic emergency"

After the COVID-19 pandemic, WHO member states agreed a package of amendments to the IHR by consensus at the World Health Assembly in 2024. They came into force on 19 September 2025.

The most visible change is a new, higher level of global alert, a "pandemic emergency". According to the WHO, it is designed to trigger stronger international collaboration when a health risk escalates beyond a PHEIC and poses the risk of becoming, or has already become, a pandemic, with widespread impact on health systems and disruption to societies.

The amendments also require governments to establish National IHR Authorities to coordinate how they implement the regulations, and include provisions to improve access to medical products and financing based on "equity and solidarity". The WHO said 11 of the 196 States Parties rejected the 2024 amendments. For them, the previous version of the IHR still applies, although they can withdraw their rejection at any time.

Separately, WHO member states adopted a WHO Pandemic Agreement at the 2025 World Health Assembly. At the time of the WHO's announcement, they were still negotiating an annex on sharing pathogens and the benefits that come from them, such as vaccines.

What the WHO cannot do

The WHO is explicit on this point: under the IHR it serves as the secretariat, "without authority to compel action by countries". States keep the sovereign right to set their own health laws and policies. A declaration carries moral and political weight and sets out an agreed international view, but national governments decide on lockdowns, border measures and vaccination.

Our analysis

A PHEIC is a tool for coordination, not command. Its value depends on countries reporting outbreaks early and honestly, which they are more likely to do if they trust that the alarm will bring help rather than punishing travel and trade restrictions. The new pandemic emergency level gives the WHO a clearer way to signal when a crisis is moving from serious to global. Whether it works will only become clear the first time it is used.
