The state pension triple lock explained, and what "adjusting" it from 2030 could mean

The full new State Pension is £241.30 a week. We explain how the triple lock decides each year's rise, why it costs so much, the new Pension Tax Allowance from April 2027, and the open questions about the government's plan to change the lock.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 5 min read

Few policies matter as much to as many people as the state pension triple lock. It decides how much the State Pension goes up each year, and it has become one of the most debated lines in the public finances. In the Budget on 28 October 2026, the Chancellor, John Healey, confirmed the government plans to "adjust" it from 2030. This explainer sets out how the lock works now, what it costs, and what we do and do not know about the change.

How much the State Pension is now

According to GOV.UK, the full rate of the new State Pension, for people who reached State Pension age on or after 6 April 2016, is £241.30 a week. Over a year that comes to £12,547.60, by our calculation.

Not everyone gets the full amount. It depends on your National Insurance record. If your record started after April 2016, you need 35 qualifying years for the full rate. People who were "contracted out" of the additional state pension before 2016 usually need more years, because they paid less into the State Pension and more into a workplace or private pension. People who would have received more under the old rules get the extra as a "protected payment" on top. You can check your own forecast on GOV.UK.

How the triple lock works

The triple lock is a promise about how the pension rises. GOV.UK says the new State Pension increases each year by whichever is highest of:

  • earnings: the average percentage growth in wages in Great Britain
  • prices: the percentage growth in prices in the UK, measured by the Consumer Prices Index (CPI)
  • 2.5%

The point is that pensioners never fall behind either workers' pay or the cost of living, and always get at least 2.5%. Protected payments are treated differently: they rise only in line with CPI.

For context, CPI inflation was 3.5% in September 2026, according to the Office for National Statistics (see our inflation explainer). The government confirms each year's increase in its annual uprating announcement. The increase then takes effect from April.

Why it is so expensive

Because the lock always takes the highest of three measures, over time the pension tends to rise faster than either prices or earnings alone. In a year when inflation spikes, pensioners get the inflation figure. In a year when wages jump, they get the earnings figure. In a quiet year, they get 2.5%. Each rise becomes the new base for the next year.

The sums are large. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) expects the government to spend £170 billion on pensioner benefits in 2026-27, roughly half of all welfare spending. Most of that is the State Pension. With an ageing population, that bill is expected to keep growing.

The tax problem, and the new Pension Tax Allowance

There is a squeeze that many pensioners have noticed. The standard Personal Allowance, the income you can receive before paying income tax, is £12,570 for 2026-27, according to GOV.UK, and the OBR notes that tax thresholds are frozen until 2030-31. The full new State Pension, at £12,547.60 a year, is now only just below that line. Another triple-lock increase would push it above, meaning people with no other income could start owing tax on their State Pension alone.

The Budget addresses this with a new Pension Tax Allowance from April 2027. According to the Treasury, people above State Pension age whose total income is no more than the value of the full new State Pension will not pay income tax. The Treasury estimates around 500,000 pensioners will benefit in 2027-28. About 290,000 will pay no income tax at all, and the rest will get a tapered allowance. The Chancellor said the allowance would apply for the rest of this Parliament.

What "adjusting" the lock from 2030 means

This is where the detail runs out. In his Budget speech, the Chancellor said: "The Prime Minister has already announced that from 2030 we will adjust the Triple Lock, so that the State Pension rises every year and keeps pace with earnings and inflation." He said this would save £15 billion a year by the end of the 2030s.

The wording suggests the pension would still rise every year and keep pace with both earnings and inflation. That implies the 2.5% floor, or the "highest of three" mechanism, could be what changes. But the government has not published a formula. Until it does, nobody can say precisely how much any individual will get after 2030.

Our analysis: questions to ask when the detail arrives

When the government sets out its plan, these are the points that will matter most to pensioners and future pensioners:

  • Is there still a floor? Without the 2.5% minimum, rises in low-inflation, low-wage years would be smaller.
  • Which measure applies each year? A rule based on the higher of earnings or prices would cost more than one based on an average or on earnings alone.
  • What happens to the base? Small differences each year add up over a retirement that may last 20 to 30 years.
  • How does it interact with tax? If thresholds stay frozen while the pension rises, the Pension Tax Allowance becomes more important. That allowance is currently promised only for this Parliament.

What you can do now

  • Check your State Pension forecast and National Insurance record on GOV.UK. Gaps can sometimes be filled with voluntary contributions, but get guidance first, because it is not worthwhile for everyone.
  • If you are close to the tax threshold, keep an eye on the detailed rules for the Pension Tax Allowance when HMRC publishes them.
