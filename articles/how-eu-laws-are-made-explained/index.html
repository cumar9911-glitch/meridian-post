How EU laws are made: a guide for UK readers

The UK left the EU, but EU rules still shape what British businesses sell into Europe's single market. We explain who proposes EU laws, how the Parliament and Council agree them, and the difference between a regulation and a directive.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

Britain left the European Union in 2020, but EU decisions still matter on this side of the Channel. Any UK business that exports to the EU has to meet EU product, food and data rules, and debates about the UK's future relationship with Europe often turn on whether Britain should follow particular EU standards. Yet the way the EU makes laws is often misunderstood, including by people who once lived under it. This guide explains the process, based on the EU's own official explanations.

The three institutions that matter

The EU's official guide names three main institutions involved in law-making:

  • The European Commission, which represents the EU's overall interests. It drafts proposals for new laws.
  • The European Parliament, which represents EU citizens and is directly elected.
  • The Council of the European Union, which represents the governments of member states. Ministers from each country meet in it, grouped by subject.

A common confusion is worth clearing up. The Council of the European Union is not the same as the European Council, which is the summit of EU heads of state or government. It is also not the Council of Europe, a separate human rights organisation that the UK still belongs to.

Step 1: the Commission proposes

Most EU laws begin with a proposal from the Commission. Before it proposes a new initiative, the EU says, the Commission assesses its likely economic, social and environmental impact in a report called an impact assessment. That report sets out the advantages and disadvantages of different policy options and draws on input from non-governmental organisations, national authorities, industry and expert groups.

Members of the public, businesses and organisations can respond to consultations and calls for evidence through the Commission's "Have your say" portal. The Commission also takes account of evaluations of existing laws and holds "implementation dialogues" with industry, small businesses, unions, employers and regional and local authorities.

National parliaments also have a formal role at this stage. They can object, or issue an opinion, if they think an issue would be better dealt with at national rather than EU level.

Step 2: Parliament and Council negotiate

Most EU policies are decided through the ordinary legislative procedure, in which the Parliament and the Council act as co-legislators. Both review the Commission's proposal and can propose amendments. In practice, the EU explains, the Parliament, the Council and the Commission then meet to see whether they can agree a complete set of amendments. These three-way talks are widely known as "trilogues".

A few rules shape the balance of power:

  • The Council can overrule the Commission's objections only by a unanimous decision.
  • The Commission can withdraw a proposal if it thinks the Parliament has changed it too much.
  • If the institutions cannot agree a final text, there is a second reading, in which further amendments are possible and the Parliament can block the proposal.
  • If they still disagree, a conciliation committee tries to find a solution. Both the Parliament and the Council can block the proposal at this final stage.

A proposal becomes law when the Parliament and the Council agree a joint text, and it is published in the Official Journal of the European Union.

Special procedures

In some areas the Council can adopt laws largely on its own, using one of two special legislative procedures:

  • Consent: the Parliament can accept or reject the proposal but cannot amend it.
  • Consultation: the Council must ask for the Parliament's opinion and wait for it, but does not have to follow it.

Regulation, directive, decision: what is the difference?

Once agreed, EU law can take several forms, and the difference matters a great deal for businesses.

  • Regulations are binding and apply in full across the EU straight away, without national legislation. The EU gives the example of the rules on mobile roaming charges, renewed in 2022 for another ten years.
  • Directives set a goal that every member state must achieve, but leave each country to write its own law to get there. The single-use plastics directive, which restricts items such as plastic plates, straws and cups, is one example.
  • Decisions are binding on the specific country or company they are addressed to, such as the Council's decision allowing Croatia to adopt the euro from 1 January 2023.
  • Recommendations and opinions are not legally binding. They allow EU institutions to set out a view or suggest a course of action.

How to follow a law as it is made

The EU publishes an EU Law Tracker and searchable records of legislative procedures. You can follow a proposal from planning through adoption to implementation. Citizens can also start or support a European Citizens' Initiative, which invites the Commission to propose legislation, or petition the European Parliament.

Our analysis: why this matters for the UK

For UK exporters, the important point is that EU rules are often settled years before they take effect, through a long and fairly open process. Impact assessments, consultations and draft texts are all public. A British firm selling into the EU can see changes coming and, through trade bodies or EU partners, can respond to consultations even though the UK no longer has a vote. The distinction between regulations and directives also matters. A regulation means one set of rules across the single market. A directive can mean 27 slightly different national versions.
