How the UN Security Council works, and why one veto can stop it

Fifteen members, five vetoes and the only UN body whose decisions every country must carry out. We explain how the Security Council votes, who sits on it now, and why it so often struggles to act on major wars.

By Sayid Omar, Editor · Published 16 November 2026 · 4 min read

Whenever a major conflict breaks out, from Ukraine to the Middle East, attention turns to the United Nations Security Council in New York. It is the UN body with the power to impose sanctions, authorise the use of force and send peacekeepers. It is also often criticised for failing to act. Both facts come down to how the Council is built. This explainer sets out its powers, membership and voting rules, drawing on the UN Charter and the Council's own guidance.

What the Council can do

Under the UN Charter, the Security Council has the main responsibility for maintaining international peace and security. According to the UN, its functions include:

  • investigating any dispute or situation that might lead to international friction, and recommending ways to settle it
  • deciding whether there is a threat to the peace or an act of aggression, and recommending what should be done
  • calling on UN members to apply economic sanctions and other measures that do not involve force
  • taking military action against an aggressor
  • recommending new UN members and the appointment of the Secretary-General, and, with the General Assembly, electing the judges of the International Court of Justice

What sets the Council apart is Article 25 of the Charter, under which UN members "agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council". The General Assembly, where all member states have a vote, can debate and recommend. The Security Council can make decisions that countries are obliged to follow.

Who sits on it

The Council has 15 members.

  • Five permanent members: China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States. The UN says they were given this status because of their key roles in setting up the organisation after the Second World War. Russia took over the Soviet Union's seat after 1990.
  • Ten non-permanent members, elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms. Under Article 23 of the Charter, a retiring member cannot be immediately re-elected.

The elected members for 2026 are Bahrain, Colombia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Latvia and Liberia (whose terms run to the end of 2027), and Denmark, Greece, Pakistan, Panama and Somalia (whose terms end this December). Countries due to join in January 2027 include Austria, Portugal, Trinidad and Tobago and Zimbabwe, according to the Council's presidency schedule. The UN notes that more than 50 member states have never sat on the Council.

The presidency rotates every month in English alphabetical order. In November 2026 it is held by Latvia.

How voting works

Article 27 of the Charter sets the rules. Each member has one vote.

  • Procedural matters, such as setting the agenda, need nine votes in favour.
  • All other matters, which covers anything of substance, need nine votes in favour "including the concurring votes of the permanent members".

That last phrase is the veto. If any one of the five permanent members votes against a substantive resolution, it fails, even if the other 14 members support it. The UN says all five have used the veto at one time or another.

There is an important nuance. A permanent member that does not support a resolution but does not want to block it can abstain. In long-standing Council practice, an abstention does not count as a veto, so the resolution can pass with nine votes in favour.

The Charter also says that when the Council is trying to settle a dispute peacefully under Chapter VI, a country that is a party to that dispute should abstain.

Why the Council so often stalls on big wars

The veto was a deliberate design choice. According to the UN, the Charter's authors expected the five powers to keep playing important roles in maintaining international peace and security, and gave them this special voting power for that reason. In practice, the Council works best when the five permanent members broadly agree, and is often unable to act when one of them, or a close ally, is directly involved in a conflict.

That is why you will often see a draft resolution win a large majority and still fail, or see the Council adopt a weaker text that all five can accept. It is also why discussion of a stalled crisis frequently moves to the General Assembly, where resolutions carry political weight but are generally not binding.

Non-members still have a voice

A UN member that is not on the Council can take part in its discussions, without a vote, when the Council considers that country's interests are affected. Countries that are parties to a dispute before the Council, including states that are not UN members, can also be invited to take part without a vote.

Our analysis: the reform debate

Calls to reform the Council are long-standing. The main arguments are that the permanent membership reflects the world of 1945 rather than today, that whole regions have no permanent seat, and that the veto lets a single country block action on mass atrocities. Changing the Charter, however, requires a two-thirds vote in the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of UN members, including all five permanent members (Article 108), which is why reform proposals have made little progress.

For readers following a crisis, the practical lesson is to watch three things: the vote count, whether any permanent member votes no, and which countries abstain. Those numbers usually tell you more about international politics than the speeches do.
