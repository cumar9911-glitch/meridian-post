How to Start an Online Shop in the UK in 2026: A Beginner's Guide

By The Daily Globe editorial · 3 November 2026 · 8 min read

Advertising disclosure: This guide contains affiliate links, marked (Ad). If you start a paid Shopify plan through them, The Daily Globe receives a commission from Shopify at no extra cost to you. We have also covered the alternatives, and the facts and prices below come from the sources listed at the end. Prices and offers change, so check each provider's own page before you pay. This is general information, not financial, legal or tax advice.

You can have an online store live in an afternoon. What takes longer is choosing something people want, getting the paperwork right and checking the sums add up. This guide covers the UK basics in the order you will meet them. It makes no promises about earnings: many new shops earn little at first, and some earn nothing.

Step 1: Choose what to sell

Start with the product, not the website. A few questions help:

  • Who is it for? A specific group ("dog owners with small flats") is easier to reach than "everyone".
  • Can you make a profit after costs? Work out the price, then subtract the product cost, postage, packaging, payment fees and returns. If little is left, the idea needs changing.
  • Where does the stock come from? You can make it yourself, buy wholesale, or use dropshipping, where a supplier posts orders for you. Dropshipping needs less money up front, but margins are usually thinner and delivery times and quality are harder to control. You are still the seller and legally responsible to your customer.
  • Are there rules for it? Food, cosmetics, electricals, toys and children's products all carry safety and labelling requirements. Check GOV.UK before you buy stock.

Test demand cheaply before spending big. A small first batch, a market stall or a few listings on a marketplace will teach you more than a spreadsheet.

Step 2: Get your tax and legal basics right

Most people start as a sole trader, the simplest business structure. You can begin trading straight away without registering as one. What matters is your income:

  • The £1,000 trading allowance. If your gross trading income (your sales, before costs) is £1,000 or less in a tax year, you may not need to tell HMRC. The tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April. You still need to keep records.
  • Over £1,000. You must register for Self Assessment by 5 October after the end of the tax year in which you passed that level. Then you submit a tax return each year. HMRC works out any Income Tax and National Insurance from your profits.
  • Allowance or expenses. Above £1,000 you can either deduct the allowance or deduct your real business expenses, not both. If your costs are more than your sales, claiming expenses usually suits you better.
  • Keep records from day one. Save invoices, receipts, bank statements and a simple sales spreadsheet.
  • Marketplaces tell HMRC about sellers. Platforms such as Etsy and eBay must report sellers who make 30 or more sales of goods, or receive about £1,700 (€2,000) or more, in a calendar year. A report does not automatically mean you owe tax, but it is a good reason to keep your own records tidy.
  • VAT. You must register once your VAT taxable turnover goes over £90,000. You can register earlier if you choose.

You may also need to pay the ICO's annual data protection fee, because you will hold customers' names and addresses. For the smallest businesses it is £52 a year (£5 less by direct debit), and some businesses are exempt. The ICO has a short online self-assessment. If you are unsure about any of this, an accountant or HMRC's helpline can confirm your own position.

Step 3: Know your customers' rights

If you sell to consumers online, the distance selling rules apply. In short:

  • Before the order, give your business name and address, a clear description, the full price including taxes, delivery costs and times, and how to cancel.
  • Tell customers they can cancel up to 14 days after delivery without giving a reason. If you do not tell them, the cancellation period can stretch to 12 months. Some goods, such as items made to a customer's specification, have exceptions.
  • Deliver within 30 days unless you agree otherwise.

Step 4: Pick a platform

You have three common routes. None is best for everyone.

Shopify: all-in-one, monthly fee

Shopify is a hosted store builder: hosting, checkout, security and a dashboard come in one package. On its UK pricing page today, the Basic plan is £19 a month if you pay yearly (£25 monthly), and Grow is £49 a month yearly (£65 monthly). Shopify says there are no set-up fees. Its standard online card rate with Shopify Payments is 2% plus 25p on Basic, and it charges an extra transaction fee of up to 2% if you use an outside payment provider instead. At the time of writing, Shopify offers a 3-day free trial and then £1 a month for the first three months, but offers change, so read the current terms before you start. The monthly fee is the main downside: you pay it whether or not you sell anything.

If you want to try it, you can start a Shopify free trial (Ad) and cancel before you are charged if it is not for you.

Etsy: a marketplace with built-in shoppers

Etsy suits handmade, vintage and creative goods. It brings its own audience, but you pay per sale. For UK sellers, Etsy lists a listing fee of $0.20 (about £0.15) for four months, a 6.5% transaction fee, and a 4% plus 20p payment processing fee, plus a 0.48% regulatory operating fee. On a £100 sale, Xero's worked example puts the basic fees at £10.85 before any advertising fees or VAT on fees. The trade-off is less control over branding and customer relationships, and you compete with many similar listings.

WooCommerce: free software, you run it

WooCommerce is a free, open-source plugin for WordPress. There is no platform fee, but you pay for hosting, a domain and any paid extensions, and you handle updates and security yourself or pay someone to. Entry-level managed WooCommerce hosting from UK hosts starts at roughly £5 to £13 a month, and costs rise as your shop grows. Its own payment service, WooPayments, charges 1.5% plus 25p on UK cards. It gives the most flexibility, and it is the most work, so it suits people who are comfortable with websites or have help.

A simple rule of thumb: use Etsy to test handmade items with little set-up, use Shopify if you want your own branded shop with less technical work, and consider WooCommerce if you want maximum control and can manage the tech.

Step 5: Take payments

Customers expect to pay by card, Apple Pay or Google Pay, and many like PayPal or buy-now-pay-later options. Each method has fees, typically a percentage plus a fixed amount per sale. Those fees come out of your margin, so build them into your prices. Klarna, for example, is listed at 4.99% plus 30p on Shopify Payments, much higher than a standard card. Choose a payment provider with clear fees and decent fraud protection, and keep a note of refunds and chargebacks (when a customer disputes a payment with their bank).

Step 6: Sort out shipping and returns

Shipping is where many new shops lose money. Before you launch:

  • Weigh and measure your packed products, packaging included.
  • Compare prices from Royal Mail, Evri, DPD and parcel-comparison sites. Prices vary by size, weight and speed, and they change.
  • Decide your policy: free delivery built into the price, a flat rate, or charge by weight.
  • Be clear about delivery times and who pays for returns.

Step 7: Marketing basics that cost little

You do not need a big budget at the start, but you do need a plan.

  • Good product pages. Clear photos, honest descriptions, sizes, materials and delivery information.
  • Search basics. Give each product a clear title and description that match what people type into Google.
  • One or two social channels. Pick where your customers already spend time.
  • Email. Collect addresses with permission (UK GDPR and PECR rules apply) and send occasional useful emails. Customers you already have are cheaper to reach than new ones.
  • Reviews. Ask happy customers for honest reviews. Never write or buy fake ones, which is against the law and against platform rules.
  • Paid ads last. Wait until you know your margins, so you know what you can afford to pay to win a customer.

If you promote other companies' products and earn commission, as this guide does, UK advertising rules say you must make that clear to your audience before they act on it.

A realistic start

A sensible first month: pick one product, check it is profitable, confirm your tax position, set up a store with clear delivery and returns pages, and make your first sales to a small test audience. Then learn from the numbers. Many shops grow slowly, and some find the first idea does not work and the second one does.

If you decide Shopify is the right fit, you can try Shopify free for 3 days (Ad). You can compare it against Etsy and WooCommerce before spending anything significant.
